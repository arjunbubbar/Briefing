6. Rates & Inflation Pulse

The Federal Reserve holds its benchmark rate at 3.50–3.75%, with market pricing now ascribing a ~39% probability of a 25bp hike over the next twelve months following April’s PPI data showing wholesale prices running at 6.0% YoY. US 10-year Treasury yields rose to 4.82% on 13 May — their highest since July 2025 — as inflation-linked sell-offs persist. Rate-cut expectations have all but evaporated for 2026, with swaps traders pricing roughly 70% odds of a hike by April 2027 (Bloomberg, 13 May).

The Bank of England maintains Bank Rate at 3.75%. MPC member Megan Greene said on 11 May that upside inflation risks from the Iran conflict are the dominant concern, and she awaits further evidence before endorsing further tightening. UK inflation is forecast to peak at 3.5–4% in H2 2026, driven by energy costs that have risen ~20% since the conflict’s outbreak in late February. The UK 10-year gilt eased 4bp to 5.07% as the session progressed, but remains near multi-year highs.

  1. Structural AI data centre demand. Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon have each reaffirmed multi-year capex commitments to AI infrastructure. Nvidia’s Blackwell GPU architecture remains the de facto standard for large-scale model training and inference, with multi-quarter order backlogs across hyperscalers. (CNBC, May 2026)
  2. CUDA moat deepening. The CUDA software ecosystem now comprises millions of developers and thousands of optimised libraries. Switching costs are rising with each generation, creating durable competitive insulation against AMD and custom-silicon alternatives. Every new foundation model trained on Nvidia hardware reinforces this flywheel.
  3. Blackwell supply ramp improving margins. After H100 supply constraints weighed on gross margins in FY2025, Blackwell production scaling is expected to push data centre gross margins above 75% in FY2027. Analysts expect earnings per share to compound at 40%+ over 2026–2028, which compresses the valuation multiple relative to growth (PEG under 1x).
  4. US domestic manufacturing tailwind. The CHIPS Act and a second tranche of semiconductor subsidies incentivise US-based fabrication. Nvidia’s partnership with TSMC’s Arizona fabs reduces geopolitical exposure and qualifies the company for federal procurement preferences that are expanding as the Middle East conflict reshapes supply-chain policy.

Why now: Nvidia’s forward P/E has compressed from 50x to ~35x as earnings growth has outpaced the share price. With Q1 FY2027 results expected to beat again and the Blackwell NVL72 rack system entering volume production, the risk/reward is attractive at current levels relative to the long-run earnings trajectory.

Key risks: (1) Tighter US export controls to China — China revenues (~15% of total) could be further curtailed by the Commerce Department; (2) AMD’s MI400 series and custom silicon (Google TPUs, AWS Trainium) are gaining inference market share, threatening Nvidia’s long-term pricing power.

Bear case (1 line): At 35x forward earnings, a single guidance miss or demand air-pocket from a pause in hyperscaler buildout could trigger a 20–30% de-rating quickly.

Continental gas demand is structurally elevated post-Russia; the Iran conflict has added a new dimension of supply uncertainty. Shell’s Integrated Gas division is expected to deliver record EBIT in H1 2026.
  3. Capital return programme. Shell’s $5bn share buyback programme announced in Q1 2026, combined with a progressive dividend commitment, provides an attractive total shareholder return profile. At 11x forward earnings and a 4.5% dividend yield, the stock is priced well below intrinsic value on a through-cycle basis.
  4. Energy security policy tailwind. The UK and EU have enacted emergency energy security legislation favouring domestic and allied-nation supply. Shell’s North Sea and Norwegian assets are eligible for accelerated permitting, while its global LNG infrastructure makes it a strategic partner for European governments seeking to reduce exposure to vulnerable supply routes.

Why now: Energy equities remain deeply under-owned by institutional investors relative to their earnings power. The sector continues to trade at a 35–40% discount to the broader market on forward P/E despite generating substantially higher free cash flow yields. A re-rating towards 14–15x earnings would imply 25–35% upside from current levels.

Key risks: (1) An Iran peace deal or OPEC+ production increase could reverse oil prices sharply, compressing earnings significantly; (2) UK windfall tax risk — the government could extend or expand energy profit levies given its fiscal constraints and political pressure from energy bills.

Bear case (1 line): If Brent reverts to $75–80/bbl on a Middle East ceasefire, Shell’s earnings halve, the buyback becomes unsustainable, and the multiple contracts rather than expands.

9. Glossary

PPI (Producer Price Index)
Measures the average change in prices that domestic producers receive for their output, before goods reach consumers. Because producer costs are typically passed on to end-buyers, a rising PPI is a leading indicator of consumer inflation — and of tighter monetary policy. The US April 2026 PPI of 6.0% YoY has materially shifted Fed rate expectations.
DXY (US Dollar Index)
A real-time benchmark tracking the value of the US dollar against a basket of six major currencies, dominated by the euro (57.6% weight), followed by the Japanese yen, pound sterling, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona, and Swiss franc. A rising DXY typically exerts downward pressure on commodities priced in USD (such as gold and oil) and reflects expectations of higher or sustained US interest rates.
Gilt
A UK government bond issued by HM Treasury, equivalent to a US Treasury bond. The term originates from gilded-edge certificates historically issued by the British government. Ten-year gilt yields signal market expectations for UK monetary policy and inflation over a decade. At 5.07%, UK gilts are near their highest yield since 2008, reflecting persistent inflation concerns and fiscal uncertainty.

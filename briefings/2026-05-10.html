6. RATES & INFLATION PULSE

The Bank of England held at 4.50% in April despite UK energy price inflation jumping to +10.9% YoY in April, driven by the Strait of Hormuz supply shock adding an estimated 60–70p per litre to domestic petrol and spiking utility bills. The MPC's dilemma is acute: core CPI at 3.1% exceeds the 2% target while the economy is contracting, with Barclays and KPMG forecasting UK GDP growth of just 0.7% in 2026. Markets are pricing fewer than two BoE cuts through year-end — a sharp reversal from the four cuts priced in January. Across the Atlantic, the Fed also stood pat at 4.25–4.50%, with Q1 US GDP rebounding to +2.0% annualised, partly cushioned by the US being a net energy exporter. Gilt yields at 4.82% (10Y) embed an elevated inflation premium; real yields remain positive but the curve is flattening as near-term rate cut hopes fade.

🇬🇧 UK: BP plc (LSE: BP.) — The Energy War Dividend

BP has become the unlikely beneficiary of the Strait of Hormuz crisis — profits doubled in a single quarter, yet the stock still trades at a steep discount to intrinsic value as investors fret over peak-oil transition risks they may be misapplying to a world now very short of hydrocarbons.

Bull case — four reasons to own BP now:

  1. Earnings inflection already happened. Q1 2026 underlying profit hit $3.2bn — more than double year-ago levels — on exceptional oil trading and midstream performance, beating the consensus of $2.63bn. EPS came in at $1.24 vs the $0.97 expected. Revenue of $53.4bn exceeded the prior-year quarter by 38%. (Source: CNBC, 28 Apr 2026)
  2. Brent at $100+ provides a sustained earnings floor. With the Strait of Hormuz largely closed and the IEA warning of a ~14 mb/d supply disruption — the largest in oil market history — Brent crude is anchored above $100/bbl absent a durable peace settlement. Even at $80 Brent, BP's new cash-generation model funds the dividend and buybacks; at $100 it throws off substantial surplus capital. (Source: IEA; Fortune/Yahoo Finance)
  3. Valuation disconnect is glaring. At ~8× forward earnings, BP trades at a deep discount to the S&P 500 (~19×) and below its supermajor peers TotalEnergies (~9×) and ExxonMobil (~14×). The Motley Fool UK notes the stock "is still treated as an oil bet" — meaning green-transition discount — even as the world has re-discovered it cannot transition away from oil fast enough to offset a supply shock of this magnitude. (Source: The Motley Fool UK, 6 May 2026)
  4. Capital return programme accelerating. Shell announced a $3.5bn buyback alongside its 23% earnings jump, and BP is expected to follow with an enhanced buyback in its next trading update. A 4.4% dividend yield covered 3.0× by free cash flow means income investors are being paid to wait. (Source: ABC Money; Invezz, 28 Apr 2026)

Why now: The 32% YTD rally has priced in higher oil but not the duration of the Hormuz disruption, which structural factors (damaged Iranian export infrastructure, ongoing US-Iran negotiations complexity) suggest will persist well into H2 2026.

Key risks: (1) A surprise ceasefire and rapid Hormuz reopening could collapse Brent toward $70–75, compressing earnings sharply; (2) UK windfall tax extensions or higher upstream levies from a revenue-hungry HM Treasury could erode post-tax returns.

Bear in one line: Any credible Hormuz reopening deal sends Brent below $80 and strips out at least a third of BP's current earnings power.

Bull case — four reasons to own XOM now:

Unlike European peers, ExxonMobil benefits directly from the Hormuz supply squeeze: US LNG and crude exports to Asia and Europe are commanding record premiums as buyers redirect away from Iranian and Persian Gulf routes. This operational leverage means earnings grow faster than the oil price at the margin.
  3. Dividend aristocrat with 40+ years of consecutive growth. XOM has not cut its dividend in over four decades, and with Brent at $100+ free cash flow comfortably covers both the dividend and an aggressive $20bn buyback programme. The 3.3% yield is notably higher than the S&P 500 average (~1.4%).
  4. Materials and energy earnings upgrades. BlackRock specifically flagged that "materials and energy have seen earnings upgrades as the Mideast conflict has pushed prices higher." Consensus 2026 EPS for XOM has been revised up ~18% YTD, yet the stock has lagged the revision, implying further re-rating potential. (Source: BlackRock BII; Seeking Alpha, May 2026)

Why now: The market is still pricing a mean-reversion to $75 Brent that requires a peace deal no senior Iranian military official is currently signalling. XOM's valuation is pricing in sub-$90 oil; structural supply disruption argues for substantially longer duration at $100+.

Key risks: (1) A rapid ceasefire and Hormuz reopening would collapse the geopolitical premium and trigger sector rotation out of energy; (2) US recession risk (GDP was +2.0% in Q1 but inflation could force the Fed to tighten, dampening demand).

Bear in one line: Peace breaks out and Brent collapses — XOM sheds 25–30% as the war premium evaporates.

9. GLOSSARY

Strait of Hormuz
A narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which roughly 20% of globally traded oil passes. Its closure or disruption can cause significant supply shocks, as seen in the 2026 Iran conflict when the IEA estimated ~14 million barrels per day were removed from global supply.
TTF (Title Transfer Facility) Gas
The benchmark European natural gas price, traded in €/MWh at a virtual hub in the Netherlands. Because Europe imports substantial LNG and pipeline gas that can be disrupted by Middle East conflicts, TTF is highly sensitive to geopolitical shocks — it surged when the Hormuz crisis began and remains elevated as alternative supply routes are constrained.
Fwd P/E (Forward Price-to-Earnings)
A valuation ratio calculated by dividing a company's current share price by its expected earnings per share over the next 12 months. A low Fwd P/E (e.g. BP at ~8×) can signal that a stock is cheap relative to its earnings power — though it may also reflect genuine risks the market is pricing in, such as energy transition uncertainty or commodity price mean-reversion.

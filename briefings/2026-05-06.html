8. STOCK DEEP DIVE

US: Exxon Mobil (XOM) — Bull Case

Exxon Mobil is the world's largest publicly traded energy company by market cap, with integrated upstream, refining, and chemicals operations across more than 60 countries. At $113/barrel Brent — a four-year high — XOM's free cash flow generation is near-record, yet the stock continues to trade at a modest forward multiple relative to the cash it is throwing off.

Price (May 6 close)~$133
Market Cap~$535B
Fwd P/E (2026E)~13.5×
Dividend Yield~3.8%

Bull Points:

  1. Record-high Brent sustains exceptional FCF. At $113/barrel, XOM's 2026 free cash flow yield is tracking above 8%, well above its 5-year average. The Pioneer Natural Resources acquisition expanded XOM's Permian Basin footprint, lowering average breakeven costs to approximately $35/barrel — meaning at today's prices, each barrel yields roughly $78 in gross margin. (Source: Fortune, XOM investor relations)
  2. Structural Permian growth. Permian output is set to exceed 1.6 million barrels per day in 2026 following Pioneer integration milestones, adding incremental low-cost volumes that incumbents cannot easily replicate. The company retains years of top-tier drilling inventory at current rig counts. (Source: Reuters)
  3. $20B annual share buyback programme. Management reaffirmed the $20B annual buyback in Q1 2026 results, representing approximately 3.7% of current market cap. Combined with dividends, total capital returns exceed 7.5% of market cap annually — a rare combination among large-caps. (Source: XOM Q1 2026 earnings)
  4. Dividend Aristocrat credibility. XOM has raised its dividend for over 40 consecutive years, underpinning pension-fund and income-investor demand as a core portfolio holding. With payout ratios manageable at high oil prices, further dividend growth is highly probable in 2026. (Source: Bloomberg)

Why Now: The market has partially de-rated energy names on transition risk and demand uncertainty, leaving XOM at a discount to intrinsic value at current oil prices. With Strait of Hormuz disruption keeping supply structurally tight and summer driving season demand approaching, the near-term setup favours holding or adding.

Key Risks: (1) Rapid diplomatic resolution to the Hormuz crisis could see Brent correct sharply toward $85–90, compressing FCF. (2) US recession fears — which would compress global oil demand — could emerge if ISM Services data deteriorates materially in coming months.

Bear in One Line: If Middle East tensions resolve and Chinese demand disappoints, Brent could revert to $85, cutting XOM's FCF yield in half and triggering a sector de-rating.

High oil and gas prices — Brent at $113 and TTF at €46/MWh — are simultaneously boosting both its upstream and LNG trading divisions, a rare double-tailwind.

Key Risks: (1) UK windfall taxes — a renewed political debate around excess profits at high oil prices could return, especially ahead of any snap election. (2) Energy transition write-downs on legacy exploration assets if long-run oil demand forecasts are revised down.

Bear in One Line: A windfall tax re-run and oil-price correction below $80/barrel would simultaneously compress earnings and strip away the buyback yield, removing both fundamental and capital-return props.

9. GLOSSARY

Term Plain English
Strait of Hormuz A narrow waterway between Iran and Oman, roughly 33 km wide at its narrowest point, through which approximately 20% of the world's seaborne oil supply passes each day. Any closure or disruption sends global oil prices sharply higher because alternative supply routes take weeks to bring online.
ISM Services PMI A monthly survey of purchasing managers at US non-manufacturing (service-sector) companies, published by the Institute for Supply Management. A reading above 50 signals expansion; below 50 signals contraction. Because services make up roughly 80% of the US economy, this index is one of the most watched leading indicators for US GDP growth.
Gilt yield The annual percentage return an investor earns from holding a UK government bond (called a "gilt" because the original certificates had gilded edges). When investors sell gilts, prices fall and yields rise. A rising gilt yield signals either inflation fears, fiscal concerns, or both — it also raises borrowing costs across the economy, from mortgages to corporate loans.
